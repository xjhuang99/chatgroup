How Using AI Makes You Less Trusting of Others

Personally using generative AI erodes trust in human counterparts, through attributional ambiguity

The Mechanism
You use AI

On an ability- or benevolence-based task

Attributional ambiguity

You expect the counterpart to credit the AI, not you

Negative meta-perceptions

You anticipate looking lower in ability & benevolence

Reduced trust in the counterpart

You defensively withhold willingness to be vulnerable

Conclusion: AI offers efficiency but introduces a relational cost, quietly eroding trust in collaborators across both ability- and benevolence-based work.

Methods

Scope & scale

8 studies (5 main, 3 supplemental)
Total N = 4,994

Domains

Ability-based (e.g., conducting market analyses)
Benevolence-based (e.g., sending sympathy emails)

Paradigms

Incentivized economic trust game
Experimental scenarios

Implications

The actor's perspective

Prior work mainly focuses either on users' trust of AI or users' trust of others using AI. This work shifts the lens to how personally using AI shapes the user's own trust in others.

AI vs. human help

Using AI creates larger attributional concerns than receiving help from another human.

Organizational governance

Establishing clear policies and norms for AI use can preserve collaborative trust.

What Buffers The Effect

Counterpart also uses AI

Reciprocal use normalizes it (Study 2).

Policy permits AI

Sanctioned use reduces threat (Study 3b).
